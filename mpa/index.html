They Still Make Great Movies: An Informal Analysis of Over 100,000 Films
Peter Janecek • October 11, 2026
The tendency for nostalgia to out-shine genuine critique is almost obligatory. As societal tastes change, older generations believe themselves to be in a perpetual state of “being left behind.” The younger generations, often immature, re-align the world to their own values, inciting ridicule from the older generations.

The common complaint among movie-goers in the 21st century is simply that “they don’t make good movies anymore.” While the majority of those who share this opinion don’t mean it literally, the common sentiment is that the golden age of cinema has long since passed. To be sure, the classical meaning of “the golden age of hollywood” did in fact end after the 60s with the advent of TV and soap-operas, however most will agree that the golden age of cinema did not die until the 00’s.

How can we be certain that this nihilistic view is a belief held across society, rather than a vocal minority? Is it even a valid argument? To answer this, I did some investigating.
I
People Have a Negative Opinion of the Modern Film Inudstry

First, we need a data source. In general, we would like to analyze the sentiment of the people, rather than of the critics. This is why I chose to use data from the film discussion and reviewing site, Letterboxd. Letterboxd has a broad range of users; ranging from the faux-academic, to the common film-buff. This will make it easy to capture popular film discourse through both the lens of so-called objectivity, and openly emotional takes.

Letterboxd, due to technical constraints, does not give its data freely. Therefore, the data we will be using will have been from a scrape of the site conducted in mid-2025. This scrape included over 800,000 films on the site, 100,000 of which are popular enough to contain usable data. To reduce noise and incompleteness, we will also be narrowing our gaze to films released from 1930 to 2024.

I wrote a program that takes our data, cleans it, and then outputs something we can use. After a few weeks, the results are in.

Average rating of films released each year from 1930 to 2024.

Unsurprisingly, the films released from 1945 to 1980 are far higher-rated. There is even a noticeable decline in average ratings from the year 1960-onward, corresponding with the end of the golden age of Hollywood. This suggests that the sentiment that “they don’t make good movies any more” was a commonly-held belief far before the post-modern era.

However, this is only a simple-average. More films are released every year, and the amount is accelerating. In turn, more “bad” films are released every year, meaning that the negative trend may be due to the sheer volume of films (good and bad) that are released every year.

The total number of films released every year from 1930 to 2024.

We can see that the negative trend in our data conveniently correlates with the volume of our data. To account for this, we will have to weight each film by how popular they are. This in itself will be a challenge.

Letterboxd doesn’t have what you might call a “popularity score”. There is a number showing how many people have logged a film, however our data source doesn’t include this metric. What it does include is a collection of the most popular reviews of each film, generally 10 or fewer. It also includes the number of likes for each review. So to create my own popularity score, I simply averaged the number of likes of each comment on each film. More popular films should have a higher value, and less popular films should have a lower value. Because we are already sorting by year, we can account for the fact that older films are less-often reviewed.

Average rating of films released each year from 1930 to 2024. Includes unweighted and weighted data.

We can now see that the decline in ratings since 1960 is not a fluke. We can also see that the difference in ratings between a film from the 1950s and a film from the 1990s is mostly explainable when we consider the now extremely pronounced 2000s trend. What previously looked like a continuation in the post-golden-age down-turn, is now a steep cliff.

This is the clearest evidence that “they don’t make good movies anymore” is the majority stance. Is this stance, however, a true reflection of the quality of modern films, or is it simply a by-product of nostalgia?

II
Why People Have This Opinion

Obviously this is a question with an unfalsifiable answer. We have been reading the previous trends as prescriptive rather than descriptive. That is to say, my analysis was predicated on the data being people’s reaction to film, rather than the actual artistic merit of the films themselves. A descriptive analysis of the data would be exactly counter to my argument, and it would not be impossible to make the case that it is the only correct reading of the data.

Both art and art criticism are entirely subjective, so the only objective trends we can pull from are people's self-proported feelings. The statement “X movie is better than Y movie” is meaningless. The statement “I believe X movie is better than Y movie” is not meaningless. The way one decides if a film is “good” or “bad” is a combination of how the community at large feels about it, and how you feel about it as an individual. This is why, I believe, the only correct way to view the data is prescriptively.

If the only objective data we can pull from is based on emotion (which itself is subjective), and that data shows that films have only decreased in quality, how can I claim that this is a misconception?

The statistics we have been discussing prove that there is a real negative outlook on the modern film industry, and it's not just from a vocal minority. However, to determine if the statement “they don’t make good movies anymore” is truthful, we need to focus our gaze on “great movies”. Our definition of what a “great” film will be very simple, and slightly arbitrary. Anything with an average rating of 4.0 or higher is a “great” film.

Total number of films released each year that have a rating of 4.0 or higher from 1930 to 2024.

While the total number of films that are rated 4.0 and over is a small portion of our data set (roughly 2.6%), the majority were released after 2000. This shows that not only do they still make “great movies”, but the amount is accelerating. Before we get ahead of ourselves, lets adjust it for the total number of films released each year.

Percentage of films released each year that have an average rating of 4.0 or higher from 1930 to 2024.

Obviously, if the total number of films released each year is accelerating, so should the amount of good films. Simultaneously, so should the amount of bad films.

In a 2013 study, the Bureau of Economic Activity (BEA) found that the film industry invested a total of $400 million into creating films. In 2010, it had risen to almost $20,000 million. However, we know that the total number of films released each year has accelerated to a similar degree. After accounting for inflation, the BEA found that the price to produce a single film has more-or-less leveled off since 1980.

A constant input cost, but a rising volume of films is a recipe for an influx of low-quality “bargain-bin” movies. Because the film industry has grown substantially, yet the input cost for a film has remained roughly the same since 1980, a much higher amount of “bad” films are released every year. While a 3.0 isn’t a terrible score for a film, we can plot the number of films with a 3.0 or lower to see the trend of low-quality movies since 1930.

Percentage of films released each year that have an average rating of 3.0 or lower from 1930 to 2024.

This is likely the most important reason why people have a negative attitude towards the modern film industry. These types of films are obviously less-often discussed, yet their influence on the popular-opinion of the industry as a whole is great.

III
They Still Make Great Movies

The film industry as a whole has never been larger. According to BoxOfficeMojo, it has even bounced back since pre-pandemic levels, and since climbed even higher. According to our dataset, more films are released now than ever before.

They still make great movies. While the barrier to entry to make a film is no-longer accelerating, and the amount of “bad” films is increasing as a result, this does not mean that they don’t still make great movies. Despite the occasional poor year (such as 2022), great films are released every year. In fact, the amount of good films released each year has only grown.

While our data didn’t include 2025 and 2026 films, the past two years in particular are great examples of how explosive the industry has been. In fact, most of the films from the past two years that have broken through the noise have been extremely well-received. From titans such as The Odyssey to low-budget indie films such as It Was Just an Accident, to instant classics such as One Battle After Another, great new ideas are finally getting their turn in the spot-light.

In fact, it is growing increasingly rare for “bad” films to be in the zeitgeist any more. These types of low-quality films that would have at least garnered a reputation in the ‘00s and ‘10s are more-or-less forgotten now-a-days. Films like Thunderbolts*, The Electric State, The Mandalorian and Grogu, and Five Nights at Freddy’s 2 are released, and quickly forgotten. Of course, there will always be the stand-out unforgettably terrible film that gains popularity because it is bad, such as War of the Worlds and Madame Web, but trashy movies that were made to be block-busters aren’t exactly busting the block anymore.

If you only watch big-budget films from big-budget studios with big-budget marketing, you will probably be disappointed. The formula for these formulaic films is being perfected. There is less room for error, artistic expression, and freshness. The fourth film of a trilogy is never something made for the sake of expression.

The industry has begun to recognize this, and shifted its attention to original story-telling and fresh ideas. This is especially true since the incredible reception of indie films such as Backrooms and Obsession, which directly resulted in an acceleration in this shift in paradigm. According to The Wrap, these films have made Hollywood re-evaluate their investments into high-value low-risk films. Instead of throwing hundreds of millions of dollars into a single film from an established franchise, they are beginning to realize that spending less on new ideas may garner a higher ROI.

Obviously they still make great movies. They make great movies every year, and will continue to do so. In fact, they're making more great films now than ever before.
